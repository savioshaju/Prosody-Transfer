\chapter[Morphological Processing and Cross-Lingual Word Alignment]{Morphological Processing and\\Word Alignment}
\label{chap:morph_alignment}

In cross-lingual prosody and emphasis transfer, the primary objective is to project an identified emphasis (or focus) constituent from a source utterance (English) into the corresponding syntactic constituent of the target utterance (Malayalam). Achieving this transfer requires addressing a fundamental typological divide: English is a predominantly analytic language with Subject-Verb-Object (SVO) word order and isolated function words, whereas Malayalam is an agglutinative Dravidian language characterized by SOV order, extensive case inflection, verbal compounding, and productive morphophonemic sandhi.

\section{Morphological Processing with mlmorph}
\label{sec:mlmorph_overview}

Malayalam is characterized by dense morphosyntax: semantic roots, derivational affixes, inflectional case markers, postpositions, and tense-aspect-mood markers routinely fuse into single orthographic words without whitespace. Standard neural aligners run subword tokenizers (such as mBERT's statistical WordPiece) directly on unsegmented text, breaking agglutinated words arbitrarily without regard to grammatical boundaries. In our pipeline, we introduce deterministic finite-state morphological pre-segmentation via \textbf{mlmorph} \citep{thottingal2019finite} before neural processing. By isolating compound stems and case clitics prior to embedding extraction, WordPiece operates on linguistically coherent morphemes rather than opaque agglutinative complexes.

Developed using the Helsinki Finite-State Transducer (HFST) framework, \textit{mlmorph} formalizes the lexicon, morphotactics, and phonological sandhi rules of Malayalam into computational transducers. It provides two complementary, bidirectional operations:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Morphological Analyser ($w \longrightarrow \text{lemma}\langle\text{tags}\rangle$):} Takes an inflected surface wordform and resolves it into its root lemma and structured grammatical tags (such as part-of-speech, nominal case, tense, aspect, and mood). For example, \ml{വന്നു} is analyzed into the verbal root \ml{വരുക} and past-tense tag \texttt{<v><past>}, while \ml{രാമന്} is resolved into the noun stem \ml{രാമൻ} and dative tag \texttt{<n><dative>}.
    \item \textbf{Morphological Generator ($(\text{lemma}, \text{tags}) \longrightarrow w$):} Inverts the analysis process by taking a root lemma and a sequence of grammatical feature tags to synthesize the phonologically valid surface wordform (for example, generating the nominalized form \ml{വന്നത്} from the verb root and participial derivation tags).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Applications of \textit{mlmorph} in the Pipeline}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Finding Part-of-Speech (POS) and Grammar Details:}\\
    Standard neural models can easily confuse words that look alike, or struggle to tell if a word is a noun, verb, or adjective. \textit{mlmorph} parses each Malayalam word and accurately identifies its root dictionary form (lemma) and grammar tags. This allows the system to clearly separate subject/object nouns from main verbs.

    \item \textbf{Splitting Compound Words (\textit{Samāsam} Splitting):}\\
    In Malayalam, multiple words are often joined together into a single long compound without spaces. When aligning with English, standard neural aligners fail because two or more English words correspond to only one long Malayalam word. \textit{mlmorph} splits these compounds into their individual parts before alignment:
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
        \item \ml{ശിക്ഷാനിയമം} $\longrightarrow$ \ml{ശിക്ഷ} (``penal'') $+$ \ml{നിയമം} (``code/law''). This allows multi-word English expressions like ``Penal Code'' to each connect cleanly to their respective constituent morphemes.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Stripping Case Endings for Better Word Alignment:}\\
    In Malayalam, case markers (such as locative ``in'', dative ``to'', or instrumental ``with'') are attached directly to nouns as suffixes. These attached endings alter the word shape and confuse multilingual language models. \textit{mlmorph} detects and temporarily removes these case endings, exposing the bare root word for accurate semantic matching:
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
        \item \ml{ഇന്ത്യയിൽ} (``in India'') $\longrightarrow$ base word \ml{ഇന്ത്യ} $+$ \texttt{<locative>}, which helps connect the English preposition ``in''.
        \item \ml{കൃഷ്ണന്} (``to Krishnan'') $\longrightarrow$ base word \ml{കൃഷ്ണൻ} $+$ \texttt{<dative>}, connecting to ``to Krishnan''.
        \item \ml{കത്തികൊണ്ട്} (``with a knife'') $\longrightarrow$ base word \ml{കത്തി} $+$ \texttt{<instrumental>}, connecting to ``with a knife''.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Grouping Full Phrases (Syntactic Chunking):}\\
    When emphasizing a constituent in a cleft sentence, we need to highlight the entire phrase (such as ``that elderly man'') rather than just an isolated word (``man''). Since word alignment often links only the main noun, \textit{mlmorph} inspects neighboring words to group them into a complete phrase:
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
        \item \textbf{Noun Phrases (NP):} In the sentence ``\textbf{That elderly man} arrived'', alignment may only link ``man'' to \ml{മനുഷ്യൻ}. \textit{mlmorph} identifies that \ml{ആ} is a demonstrative pointer (``that'') and \ml{വൃദ്ധനായ} is an adjective (``elderly''). It groups them together into \ml{ആ വൃദ്ധനായ മനുഷ്യൻ}, ensuring the whole phrase is emphasized.
        \item \textbf{Postpositional Phrases (PP):} In phrases like \ml{മേശപ്പുറത്ത്} (``on top of the table''), \textit{mlmorph} separates the noun \ml{മേശ} (``table'') and the position marker \ml{പുറത്ത്} (``on top of''), keeping the full phrase boundary intact.
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Joining the Focus Marker (\textit{āṇŭ}) with Proper Sandhi:}\\
    In a cleft construction, the focus word \ml{ആണ്} (\textit{āṇŭ}) attaches directly to the emphasized constituent. \textit{mlmorph} checks the end of the host word to apply the correct sound joining rules (\textit{sandhi}) and preserve necessary case markers:
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
        \item \textit{Preserving Case Endings:} \ml{ജോണിന്} $+$ \ml{ആണ്} $\to$ \ml{ജോണിനാണ്} (``It is to John''), preserving dative semantics (stripping the case marker would produce incorrect *\ml{ജോണാണ്}).
        \item \textit{Applying Proper Sandhi:} It determines whether the word boundary needs virāma deletion (\ml{നിലത്ത്} $+$ \ml{ആണ്} $\to$ \ml{നിലത്താണ്}), chillu joining (\ml{അവൻ} $+$ \ml{ആണ്} $\to$ \ml{അവനാണ്}), anusvāra change (\ml{പുസ്തകം} $+$ \ml{ആണ്} $\to$ \ml{പുസ്തകമാണ്}), or glide insertion (\ml{കുട്ടി} $+$ \ml{ആണ്} $\to$ \ml{കുട്ടിയാണ്}).
    \end{itemize}

    \item \textbf{Converting the Verb into a Nominal Form (Verb Normalisation):}\\
    When an item is focused using a cleft sentence, the main action verb must be turned into a relative noun form ending in \ml{-ത്} (\textit{-atŭ}) \citep{moag2002malayalam}. \textit{mlmorph} reads the verb's tense and mood, and generates the correct nominalized form:
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
        \item \ml{വാങ്ങി} (past: ``bought'') $\longrightarrow$ \ml{വാങ്ങിയത്} (``what was bought'')
        \item \ml{വരുന്നു} (present: ``comes'') $\longrightarrow$ \ml{വരുന്നത്} (``the one coming'')
        \item \ml{പോയില്ല} (negative: ``did not go'') $\longrightarrow$ \ml{പോകാത്തത്} (``that which did not go'')
        \item \ml{പോകണം} (modal: ``must go'') $\longrightarrow$ \ml{പോകേണ്ടത്} (``that which must be done'')
        \item \textbf{Guarding Against Double Endings:} If a verb is already in nominal form (such as \ml{വന്നത്}), \textit{mlmorph} detects this and leaves it unchanged, avoiding incorrect double endings like *\ml{വന്നതത്}.
        \item \textbf{Checking for Errors (Validation):} Newly generated forms are tested by parsing them back through \textit{mlmorph}. If a generated word is invalid or ungrammatical, \textit{mlmorph} rejects it, allowing the pipeline to safely fall back to standard rules.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Cross-Lingual Word Alignment Frameworks}
\label{sec:alignment_frameworks}

Once emphatic focus is localized in the source English utterance, word
alignment is used to identify the corresponding Malayalam token or token
span. Two word alignment models were evaluated in this work: SimAlign and
Awesome-Align.

\subsection{SimAlign}

SimAlign \citep{sabet2020simalign} is an unsupervised word alignment framework that extracts word alignments from multilingual language models without requiring parallel training data. In this work, SimAlign was evaluated with multilingual BERT (mBERT) Layer~8 embeddings using the \textit{IterMax} method.

\subsection{Awesome-Align}

Awesome-Align \citep{dou2021word} is a neural word alignment framework that extracts word alignments from multilingual language models using self-attention and cross-lingual objective formulations. In this work, the \textbf{zero-shot mBERT (Vanilla)} checkpoint was evaluated without bilingual parallel text fine-tuning. Awesome-Align computes forward ($E \to M$) and backward ($M \to E$) conditional probability matrices from neural layer representations, extracting word alignment pairs via the \textbf{bidirectional intersection} of links exceeding a threshold $c$. 

Crucially, while the numerical probability threshold ($c = 0.001$) is nominally very permissive, the dropped links in Malayalam do not stem from the threshold being strict; rather, they arise from the \textbf{strict bidirectional intersection requirement} ($\text{forward} \cap \text{backward}$). For an alignment link $(i, j)$ to survive, it must simultaneously satisfy $P(j|i) > c$ and $P(i|j) > c$. When word orders diverge sharply (such as Malayalam SOV vs.\ English SVO) or when grammatical relations are asymmetric (e.g., adverbs, auxiliaries, light verbs), the probability distribution in one direction is often diffused across the clause. This failure of mutual agreement causes the intersection to discard asymmetric links entirely, resulting in sparse, precision-oriented alignments with frequent token omissions in Malayalam.

\section[Morpho-SimAlign: Segmented Alignment]{Morpho-SimAlign: Segmented Alignment}
\label{sec:morpho_simalign}

While standard SimAlign provides substantial recall gains over Awesome-Align, evaluating simple SimAlign on raw Malayalam text exposes two persistent morphological bottlenecks:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Compound Atomicity:}\\
    In Malayalam, multi-morpheme compounds such as \ml{ശിക്ഷാനിയമം} (``Penal Code'') are written as single unbroken tokens. Plain SimAlign treats each compound as an indivisible unit; consequently, multi-word English source phrases compete for a single target token, frequently causing one constituent word (e.g., ``Code'') to be dropped or misaligned.
    \item \textbf{Preposition--Noun Competition on Inflected Forms:}\\
    English expresses spatial and argument relations through free prepositions (e.g., \textit{in, to}), whereas Malayalam primarily uses bound inflectional case suffixes (e.g., locative \ml{-യിൽ}, dative \ml{-ിന്}). In raw neural models, the English preposition frequently links to the entire case-inflected noun token (e.g., \textit{in} $\leftrightarrow$ \ml{സപ്ലൈകളിൽ} in Table~\ref{tab:comparison_sent3}). Because the unsegmented token cannot easily accommodate multiple argmax links, this preposition link can crowd out the English head noun itself, leaving content words like \textit{supplies} unaligned.
\end{enumerate}

To address compound splitting and preposition binding, we developed the \textbf{Morpho-SimAlign} framework, integrating \textit{mlmorph} with SimAlign.



\subsection{Architectural Pipeline of Morpho-SimAlign}
Figure~\ref{fig:morpho_pipeline} shows the four steps in Morpho-SimAlign:

\caption{The Four Steps of Morpho-SimAlign}
\label{fig:morpho_pipeline}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Splitting and Stripping with mlmorph:}\\
    Each Malayalam word is decomposed by \textit{mlmorph}. Compound words are separated into distinct stems (such as \ml{ശിക്ഷ} $+$ \ml{നിയമം}), and inflectional case endings are isolated so that the base roots can be presented cleanly to the aligner.
    
    \item \textbf{Neural Alignment on Base Words:}\\
    SimAlign aligns the English tokens with the decomposed Malayalam stems. Because the roots are exposed, multi-word English terms (like ``Penal'' and ``Code'') map directly to their corresponding morphemes.

    \item \textbf{Mapping Back to Original Words:}\\
    Each alignment link established on a split morpheme is automatically remapped to the index of the original host token in the unsegmented sentence.

    \item \textbf{Linking English Prepositions to Case Markers:}\\
    English prepositions are systematically linked to Malayalam nouns exhibiting the corresponding case features:
    \begin{itemize}[noitemsep]
        \item \textit{in, at, on} $\longleftrightarrow$ Locative case (\texttt{<locative>}, e.g., \ml{ഇന്ത്യയിൽ})
        \item \textit{to, for} $\longleftrightarrow$ Dative case (\texttt{<dative>}, e.g., \ml{ജോണിന്})
        \item \textit{with, by} $\longleftrightarrow$ Instrumental case (\texttt{<instrumental>}, e.g., \ml{കത്തികൊണ്ട്})
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Morpho-SimAlign}
\label{sec:morpho_simalign_eval}

\textbf{Key Empirical Findings and Limitations:}\\
A rigorous inspection of Table~\ref{tab:comparison_morpho} reveals both the specific benefits and the clear empirical regressions introduced by morphological pre-segmentation:
\begin{enumerate}[noitemsep]
    \item \textbf{Genuine Gain on Compound Heads:} The clear empirical advantage of Morpho-SimAlign is resolving compound head-word omission. In raw SimAlign, the unbroken compound \ml{ശിക്ഷാനിയമം} is consumed by \textit{Penal}, leaving \textit{Code} completely unaligned. Splitting the compound into constituent stems (\ml{ശിക്ഷ} and \ml{നിയമം}) allows both English tokens to ground on their respective morphemes and map back to \ml{ശിക്ഷാനിയമം}.
    \item \textbf{Empirical Regressions on Conceptual Compounds:} Pre-segmentation does not produce uniform gains across the sentence. For holistic conceptual compounds like \textit{homosexuality}, raw SimAlign successfully produces the correct link to \ml{സ്വവർഗ്ഗരതി}. In Morpho-SimAlign, splitting the word into \ml{സ്വവർഗ്ഗ} (``same-sex'') and \ml{രതി} (``sexuality/coitus'') disperses the semantic representation across two isolated sub-tokens, causing similarity to drop below the IterMax threshold and resulting in a \textbf{complete drop / regression}.
    \item \textbf{Linguistic Pitfalls of Subword Matching (\textit{crime} vs.\ \ml{കുറ്റകരം}):} In Malayalam, the adjective \ml{കുറ്റകരം} (``criminal, punishable'') is derived from the nominal root \ml{കുറ്റ-} (``crime, guilt'') plus the productive suffix \ml{-കരം} (``causing''). If an unsupervised aligner links the English noun \textit{crime} to \ml{കരം}, this is linguistically a \textbf{spurious false match}: the independent noun \ml{കരം} means ``tax'' or ``hand'', while the semantic locus of ``crime'' resides strictly in \ml{കുറ്റ-}. This demonstrates that unsupervised morphological decomposition can introduce spurious subword attributions without external semantic verification.
    \item \textbf{Heuristic Limits on Lexical Postpositions:} While the case-binding heuristic successfully anchors prepositions like \textit{in} to locative-marked nouns (\ml{ഇന്ത്യയിൽ}), it is strictly restricted to bound inflectional clitics. It cannot handle free lexical postpositions like \ml{പ്രകാരം} (``under / according to''). As a result, while raw SimAlign leveraged broad contextual embeddings to align \textit{Under} $\leftrightarrow$ \ml{പ്രകാരം}, Morpho-SimAlign leaves \textit{Under} \textbf{unaligned} (a second notable regression).
\end{enumerate}
